# The Cigar Galaxy · M82 (NGC 3034)

> "A silver pipe slanting across twelve million light-years of void, venting the crimson fury of a stellar burst into deep space."

---

## Celestial Profile

- **Catalog Identifiers**: The Cigar Galaxy · Messier 82 / M82 / NGC 3034 / 3C 231
- **Object Type**: Starburst galaxy (viewed edge-on)
- **Constellation**: Ursa Major (near the Big Dipper)
- **Physical Scale**: ~5 times the luminosity of the Milky Way, yet compressed into a diameter of only 37,000 light-years
- **Distance from Earth**: ~12 million light-years (core member of the M81 Galaxy Group)
- **Physical Mechanism**: A close gravitational passage by its massive neighbor M81 roughly 100 million years ago sent tidal shocks compressing cold gas into M82's core, sparking starbirth at ten times the rate of our galaxy. The collective detonation of tens of thousands of supernovae has breached the disk, venting a twenty-thousand-light-year "supergalactic wind" of ionized hydrogen.

## The Story: The Gravitational Tempest of M82

Twelve million light-years out toward Ursa Major, two titans dance across the dark: M81 and M82.

M81 is an archetype of cosmic poise—a grand-design spiral uncoiling its arms in stately geometric symmetry. But right beside it, M82 presents a spectacle of untamed kinetic violence. Viewed from Earth, it slants edge-on like a lit silver cigar glowing in the cold.

Roughly one hundred million years ago, M82 slipped past the massive flank of M81. They never physically collided, but invisible gravitational tides surged through M82 like a tsunami. Gas that once drifted in lazy orbits was slammed inward, compressed relentlessly toward a central core barely a few thousand light-years across.

Under that crushing pressure, star formation erupted at a pace ten times greater than in our own Milky Way. Tens of thousands of massive blue giants were born in the same cosmic instant, burned through their nuclear reserves in a few million years, and detonated in rapid-fire supernovae.

The combined shockwaves broke through the galactic disk. Escaping the gravitational well, millions of solar masses of hot, ionized hydrogen were blasted out in a colossal "supergalactic wind." Billowing outward at millions of kilometers per hour, glowing tendrils of crimson gas span over twenty thousand light-years above and below the galactic plane.

In NASA's ultra-high-resolution 8,000-pixel composite, the central disk shines with icy, steel-blue serenity, while perpendicular fountains of vermilion smoke erupt into the void. It is not destruction; it is the most visceral scream of vitality gravity can command.
